### Propensity estimation and weighting

`S1_01_01_residential_data_preparation.ipynb` fits five-fold cross-fitted logistic propensity models on the complete-case sensitivity samples, with C=1, categorical covariates, a joint state–year indicator and state–year-by-covariate terms. Each episode receives a score from a model fitted without its fold. Approved folds and row ordering are retained for reproducibility. The eligible exports retain missing covariates and do not carry final imputation-specific scores.

For estimated probability e of long-term care, the overlap weight is **1−e for long-term episodes and e for short-term episodes**. Episodes in poorly supported propensity tails contribute less. The current workflow performs **no individual propensity trimming, score clipping or weight winsorization**. Earlier state–year restrictions are sample exclusions and are reported separately.

The operational notebooks save [residential completion](outputs/S1_01_data_preparation_20260921_132051_609963/completion_propensity_eligible_draw1.png), [residential frequency](outputs/S1_01_data_preparation_20260921_132051_609963/frequency_propensity_eligible_draw1.png), [14-state outpatient completion](outputs/S1_03_outpatient_data_preparation_20260921_144945_057424/completion_propensity_eligible_draw1.png), and [14-state outpatient frequency](outputs/S1_03_outpatient_data_preparation_20260921_144945_057424/frequency_propensity_eligible_draw1.png) propensity figures from the **first saved validation draw of each fixed eligible sample**. Each figure has two panels: the left shows the arm-specific distribution of the estimated treatment probability before weighting; the right shows the same episodes after overlap weighting. Curves are normalized within arm, so their height does not show arm size. Dotted lines at 0.05 and 0.95 identify descriptive tails, not trimming thresholds. These figures show where both arms contribute and how weighting changes the target population. Similar weighted curves are expected from propensity-based weights; the figures alone cannot establish local balance, adequate effective sample size, correct imputation, or causal identification. The numeric balance and state–year checks below remain necessary.

After reconstruction, the code verifies exact record membership, covariate/outcome hashes, fold alignment and agreement with reference propensity scores. Exported samples are reloaded and checked. Reference IDs verify selection after applying the rules; they do not replace selection from raw fields.

### Treatment alternatives and the outpatient candidate

The four comparisons discussed were LOS >30 versus ≤30 days, long-term versus short-term residential care, intensive versus non-intensive outpatient care, and medication planning. Residential care remains the frozen S1_01 design. LOS raised exposure-timing and support concerns; medication planning raised measurement concerns. Outpatient intensity was initially deferred, then approved for a **separate** sample and overlap review in S1_03. Neither treatment definition inherits the other's population or outcome estimates.

**LOS >30 versus ≤30 days: set aside because local support remained problematic and the exposure requires a separate duration-based justification.** In the archived ESS50 comparison, long-term residential episodes had 19 state–year cells with local SMD above 0.10, and non-intensive outpatient episodes had 33, despite pooled maximum SMDs of 0.0080212573715852 and 0.0045914582719617. These are the earlier LOS diagnostic counts, whose local-SMD rule differs from the current joint gap/SMD rule. They show why pooled balance alone was insufficient. LOS also accumulates during an episode, so a causal interpretation must address its timing relative to completion and discharge outcomes. The saved review does not establish that 30 days is preferable to 20 or 40 days. We therefore prioritized recorded residential service category. This changes the treatment question; it does not resolve the duration design by relabeling it.

Evidence: [earlier LOS support comparison](helper/assets/evidence/S1_2/support_comparison/summary.csv).

A separate tree trained and built only on 2022 episodes gains 2.03 points when evaluated on all 182,750 eligible 2023 episodes, with a 1.44-point descriptive state jackknife standard error. A post-hoc sensitivity tree fills missing policy inputs using 2022 training-set modes and gains 2.30 points on the same 2023 episodes, with a 1.52-point descriptive standard error. The two temporal trees assign different treatments to 38,141 episodes (20.9%), so similar average values do not imply a stable individual rule. Terminal-node stars use independently held-out effects with state-clustered Student-t tests and Holm adjustment within each tree; they do not test split selection or overall policy value. Several state-specific gains are negative. Missing-value patterns can still define eligible-sample splits; mode filling removes explicit missing-category splits but does not recover unobserved values. These are internal observational diagnostics, without validated overall policy inference or evidence of a clinically transferable rule.

**Stage 1 completion inference check, September 22.** The [S1_03 notebook](S1_03_completion_inference.ipynb) separately analyzes the fixed residential and 14-state outpatient completion samples. Four constrained imputation chains produce five analyzed completed datasets each; propensity and outcome models are refitted within each dataset. The [completed run](outputs/S1_03_completion_inference_20260921_194212_249868/manifest.json) contains 40 draw estimates and source hashes, and the [timed report](reports/S1_03_completion_inference_20260922_033325_864661_full.html) records the 7-hour-51-minute execution. The adjusted recorded-completion contrasts average −15.10 percentage points for long-term versus short-term residential care and −1.85 points for intensive versus non-intensive outpatient care. Their mean unaugmented overlap-weighted contrasts are −15.02 and −2.14 points, respectively. These are exploratory associations in separate overlap populations. The outpatient estimate R-hat is 1.58 with only five saved iterations per chain, so imputation convergence is not established. The state-score standard errors (2.67 and 2.30 percentage points, respectively) condition on fitted models and cannot be used as validated causal uncertainty. The residential score tail reaches 21.69% outside [0.05, 0.95], although overlap weights downweight these episodes. This check does not validate the S1_02 policy trees or resolve outcome coding, repeated episodes, missing-at-random assumptions, or unmeasured confounding.

The archived 2022 state reporting crosswalks give different local meanings to national completion and transfer codes, including transfer after completing a current care level in Illinois and referral after substantial progress in Missouri. The [SAMHSA reporting instructions](https://www.samhsa.gov/data/data-we-collect/teds-treatment-episode-data-set/instructions) say state crosswalks are reviewed annually, but a matching public 2023 state-crosswalk set was not available in this audit. No state was excluded because of the sign of its completion contrast. Until the 2023 mappings and measurement interpretation are settled, the endpoint is described as **recorded administrative completion**, not a harmonized clinical success measure. The [2022 codebook](../../data/raw/TEDS-D-2022-DS0001-info-codebook_v1.pdf) (PDF p. 39) and [2023 codebook](../../data/raw/teds-d-2023-ds0001-info-codebook_v1.pdf) (PDF p. 38) give the same national descriptions for treatment completion and transfer. This verifies the national labels across years, but cannot verify how each state mapped its local categories in 2023.

**MOUD planning: retained as exploratory because the exposure and comparison-group coding are uncertain.** METHUSE records a medication plan, while the intended clinical question concerns receipt of medications for opioid use disorder. The saved state-crosswalk review identifies substantive differences: New York's no-plan coding can include null plan information outside opioid programs, and Texas derives the indicator from service types. These differences complicate interpretation of a pooled plan-versus-no-plan contrast even when observed covariates balance well.

The numerical diagnostics do not justify rejecting every MOUD candidate on overlap alone. For completion among short-term residential episodes, the ESS50 screen retained 19,348 of 28,172 complete cases; the saved maximum weighted SMD was 0.0192708536340267. For long-term residential completion it retained 12,468 of 18,033. For intensive-outpatient completion it retained 3,936 of 13,791, indicating a much more restricted supported population. The medication candidates were restricted to primary opioid codes SUB1=5,6,7 and evaluated within service setting. A future analysis could explicitly target the association or effect of a **recorded medication plan**, after resolving reporting and treatment-timing assumptions. It would require a different interpretation from confirmed MOUD receipt.
